# Why Do People Post on [Bad Platform] Instead of [Good Platform]?

There's a class of comment you often see when someone makes a popular thread on Mastodon, Twitter, Threads, or similar platforms:

> "Why make a Twitter thread? This would be better as a blog post."

Or stronger variants:

> "I can't read those tweets that span pages because the user puts 5 words in each reply. I find common internet completely stupid: Twitter, TikTok, Instagram, etc. What a huge waste of energy."

> "When someone chooses to blog on Twitter you know it's facile at best, and more likely simply stupid."

These comments are remarkably common. I pulled Foone's last 10 Twitter threads that scored 200+ points on Hacker News: **9 out of 10 had complaints about using Twitter instead of a blog.**

## The Bafflement

People express genuine confusion about this choice:

> "How many tweets are there just to make his point? 200? Nobody thinks 'maybe this will be more coherent on a single page'? I don't get social media."

> "Come on, typing a short description and uploading a picture 100 times is easier than typing everything in one block? Objectively speaking it is more work."

## My Own Perspective

Personally, I don't like video format. For 95% of YouTube videos I see, I'd rather have a blog post. For an interested reader, long-form blog posts are basically strictly better than long threads on social platforms.

**But I also recognize: much of the content I want to read wouldn't exist at all if it wasn't for "bad platforms."**

## The Four Main Reasons

There are four main reasons people use [bad platform]:

1. **It gets more engagement**
2. **It's where their friends are**
3. **It's lower friction**
4. **It monetizes better**

Let's examine each.

### Reason 1: Engagement

Short-form platforms like Twitter, Instagram, and TikTok completely dominate longer-form platforms like Medium and Blogspot—visible in company valuations and survey data.

Substack is the hottest platform for long-form content, yet its last valuation was ~$600M. This is a rounding error compared to short-form platform valuations.

**The money follows the people.** Most people have moved on from long-form content.

When people using Substack discuss where their readers and growth come from, the answer is platforms like Twitter. **People doing long-form content who optimize for engagement or revenue still produce lots of short-form content.**

### Reason 2: Friends and Community

People use whatever platforms others around them are using. This is generational.

I started blogging in 2013 knowing it was a dying medium relative to video—but I was already established. If I were 10 years younger starting in 2023, I'd probably try streaming before blogging.

**Generational shift is real.** While there are older video creators and younger bloggers, the distribution by age shows a clear inflection point. Video is now natural for people starting to share their thoughts online.

On microblogging platforms specifically, this compounds: people are part of communities they interact with regularly, so posting on-platform makes more sense than directing them elsewhere.

### Reason 3: Friction (The Emotional Barrier)

This is where the criticism reveals a fundamental misunderstanding.

When people say [bad platform] has lower friction, they're **not talking about the number of clicks**. Critics respond:

> "Come on, typing a short description and uploading a picture 100 times is easier than typing in one block and adding connectors? Objectively more work."

But consider: many widely-read programmer/tech bloggers I know actually use higher-friction platforms like Jekyll and Hugo. They *could* use Substack or WordPress, but they don't.

**The friction isn't technical—it's emotional.**

Many people find it mentally easier to write in short chunks than in one large block of text. This isn't necessarily rational, but it is **simply true**.

#### Foone's Explanation

Here's a concrete example. Foone, whose threads regularly go viral, explains:

> "CAUSE I CAN'T MAKE A BLOG POST, GOD DAMN IT. I have ADHD. I have bad ADHD that is being treated, and the treatment is NOT WORKING TERRIBLY WELL. I cannot focus on writing blog posts. It will not happen.
>
> If I try to make a blog post, it'll end up being abandoned and unfinished. I cannot edit it into something readable and postable. So if I went 100% to blogs: You would get no content. I would get lots of unfinished drafts and a feeling of being useless.
>
> But I can do rambly tweet threads. They don't require sustained attention. They don't have the endless editing loop. I can do them. They're just rambly and Twitter, which some people don't like."

This isn't uncommon. Three of my favorite bloggers have mentioned they can only write in one sitting—either they have momentum for an entire post or nothing.

**The gap between writing one tweet versus an entire blog post in one session is a difference of kind, not just degree.**

When someone says [bad platform] is "objectively" more work, they're measuring the wrong thing. The relevant friction is psychological, not mechanical.

### Reason 4: Revenue

Video monetizes far better than text. When video became viable, it enabled content that wouldn't exist in text form.

#### Automotive Content Example

Before YouTube, nobody was doing automotive reviews with the depth of **Alex Dykes** (Alex on Autos). Open a 1997 issue of Car and Driver or Road and Track—the depth is maybe 1/10th of what Alex does. Same for modern car magazines today.

The same applies to **Jonathan Benson** (Tyre Reviews). Before him, nobody tested tires with this breadth and depth publicly. Engineers at tire companies did this, but you had to contact them directly.

**This content wouldn't exist without video.** Not because it's impossible to describe, but because:

1. People prefer watching video to reading text
2. Video monetizes much better, making it a viable full-time job

You can make money with paywalled newsletters, but that's essentially what magazines do. Car magazines never supported anything resembling Alex Dykes's work, and it seems implausible they could support Jonathan Benson's work.

#### Tech Content Example

**Lucy Wang** (Tech With Lucy) created her channel and at 50k subscribers with thousands to tens of thousands of views, noted she was making more than she did working for AWS—mostly from sponsorships.

My blog gets over a million hits per post. I make nowhere near what Lucy made at AWS. My blog barely covers rent.

**Free text doesn't monetize well.** You can monetize text if you put most behind a paywall (like Gergely Orosz with his newsletter), but if you want mostly or exclusively free content, **video dominates text.**

## Why This Matters

People say: "this content is objectively worse when posted to Twitter." But consider what actually happens if you enforce this preference:

- The ADHD person who can only write in short form stops creating content entirely
- The video creator can't sustain their work financially
- The tire reviewer doesn't exist
- Foone's Twitter threads don't exist

Not: "They're replaced by worse blog posts." But: **They don't exist at all.**

## The Real Cost of Format Purism

I'd prefer most YouTube/Twitter/Threads/Mastodon content be on text blogs. But the reality: **most of that content wouldn't exist if forced into long-form text.**

Summary tools may eventually make this moot, but current AI summaries often get key details badly wrong.

**We have to live with content in the form it's created.**

## Beyond Format Complaints

There's a meta-reason these complaints matter less than they seem. According to David Kok's observation on bridge player complaints:

> "Rather than some wrong but meaningful statement, I always interpret statements like 'IQ has gone down' as 'I am unhappy and have difficulty expressing that.' When everyone concurs, they're expressing shared unhappiness, not legitimate reasons."

**Applied to format complaints:** Many of these aren't reasoned critiques—they're expressions of preference or frustration. The actual reasons someone doesn't write a blog post are irrelevant to someone just venting.

This doesn't make the reasons invalid, but it reframes them: some complaints are feedback, some are just aesthetic preference.

## The Engagement Optimization Loop

Most people don't consciously optimize for engagement or revenue. But there's non-conscious optimization happening.

When people first join Mastodon or Threads, they post diverse content. Most gets little traction; some does. Over time, they post less low-engagement content and more high-engagement content. **Platforms reward engagement, and it works.**

This intra-platform effect likely drives inter-platform choice too: people naturally drift toward what gets eyeballs.

## My Own Position

I'm not optimizing for engagement or revenue (though I'm supported by Patreon). My last post was 20k words. Two passes of writing took roughly 7 hours 40 minutes. The research involved reading 5 books and 15+ papers.

A while back, I tweeted daily for a few months and grew my Twitter followers by 50% (20k to 30k). That took about as much time as writing my last post—*not including* the research time.

**From engagement/revenue standpoint, short-form posting dominates.** Anyone who cares about those metrics should do it instead.

But I'm working on two long-form projects:
- One requires summarizing ~500 articles/comments for an appendix plus reading a 400-page book
- Another requires finishing notes on ~350 pages of FTC memos

Each will become a book-length blog post. I'll be lucky to make minimum wage on this, not including unpublished research.

**I'm a weirdo.**

## The Actual Outcome

Most people produce content that gets eyeballs. So of course far more people create hastily-written long Twitter threads than carefully-researched blog posts.

**That's not a bug in their judgment—it's rational choice under normal incentives.**

The question isn't why people choose "bad platforms" over "good" ones. **The question is: what kind of content do we want to incentivize?**

If we want engagement-optimized threads and viral videos, the current system works perfectly. If we want deep research and careful thought, we need different incentives.

Both have value. Most people, most of the time, will choose the former because that's where the audiences are and the money goes.

Understanding *why* requires acknowledging that the people making these choices aren't irrational—they're responding to genuine constraints: neurological, financial, and social.
